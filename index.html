About me

From an island in the Atlantic to the North Sea.

I was born and raised in Lanzarote, in the Canary Islands. I grew up by the ocean, and it's still my favourite place in the world.

I loved sport so much that at 13 I moved to Madrid to play more competitive tennis.

In 2020, in the middle of COVID, I got an academic and sports scholarship to Oklahoma Baptist University. A year later the tennis team closed because of COVID budget cuts. So I moved to New York, where I played NCAA Division II tennis and finished my degree in Business Management, with minors in Economics and Computer Science. I stayed one more year in New York to work.

After New York I spent a month at home, then four months exploring Asia. I came back to Lanzarote in December and stayed for eight months, until my master's in data science started in September. That's how I ended up in Rotterdam, and I stayed. Today I'm a Data Scientist at Danone, in the Global Quality & Food Safety team. I help run the dashboards and automations that show how regions and plants in more than 120 countries perform against their quality and food safety targets.

I speak Spanish, Russian and English, and I'm always happy to try a fourth language over coffee.

Why data

I chose data because it's the best way I know to understand people and actually help them.

01

I love understanding why

I like to analyse things and understand why they work the way they do. Finding patterns and connections that aren't obvious at first is what I enjoy most.

02

I'm curious about people

I've always wanted to know why people think and act the way they do. Data lets me answer that with evidence, not guesses.

03

I want my work to help

Good intentions aren't enough. Data shows where help is needed most, and whether it's really working.

04

It connects everything I love

The ocean, health, sport, sustainability: data is useful in all of them. It lets me work on what I care about.

🤝 Social impact

Helping UNICEF keep its donors

I built a model that predicts which donors might stop giving, so the team can reach out before they leave.

Why it matters: every donor who stays means more children UNICEF can keep supporting.

PythonRXGBoostLLM-simulated interventions
See the project on GitHub ↗
🤝 Social impact

What I found

  • Trust and the feeling of making an impact drive loyalty most. Income stops mattering once you know how donors feel.
  • 94% of donors at risk of leaving already believe in UNICEF's mission. They doubt that their money makes a difference.
  • A personal "Proof of Impact" message raised predicted loyalty (NPS) by +0.86. Bad news hurt about twice as much as good news helped.

🌱 Sustainability

What 50,000 reviews say about second-hand

My MSc thesis: how people really feel when brands start reselling their own second-hand products.

Why it matters: resale is more sustainable, and it only works if people trust it. My thesis combined psychology, data and sustainability.

PythonRSentiment analysis (VADER)Topic modelling (LDA)Synthetic difference-in-differences
See the project on GitHub ↗
🌱 Sustainability

What I found

  • On average, launching a resale channel did not make customers rate a brand higher.
  • After a launch, customers wrote more negatively about price and value.
  • Brands reacted very differently: outdoor and athletic brands lost on every measure, while premium fashion gained on sustainability.

❤️ Health

What helps people live longer?

Using global health indicators, I looked for the factors that matter most for life expectancy.

Why it matters: it shows where health policy can make the biggest difference.

RPCAElastic Net regression
See the project on GitHub ↗
❤️ Health

What I found

  • A country's economic development and health system (income, healthcare spending, clean water and sanitation) explain the biggest differences in how long people live.
  • Five themes, including chronic disease and lifestyle risks like obesity, explain about 81% of the differences between countries.
  • The best model predicts life expectancy to within about 3.3 years: 81.9 for the Netherlands, 76.7 for Colombia and 64.2 for Kenya.

❤️ Health

Does screen time make us less happy?

I predicted people's happiness from sleep, stress and social media habits.

Why it matters: mental wellbeing matters, and this project mixes psychology and data, my favourite combination.

Rk-nearest neighboursPCA
See the project on GitHub ↗
❤️ Health

What I found

  • By comparing each person with the 18 people most similar to them, the model predicted happiness to within about 1 point on a 1–10 scale.
  • Everyday habits like sleep, stress, exercise and screen time carry real signal about how people feel.
  • The model was best for happy people and tended to overestimate how happy the least happy people were. Those are exactly the people you'd most want to spot.
